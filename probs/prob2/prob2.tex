\begin{proposition}
    For any integer $k$, $k^3 - k$ is a multiple of $6$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}\nobreakspace

    Firstly, recall that an integer is a multiple of an integer $6$ if it can be expressed as 
    the product of $6$ and some other integer. Now, observe what happens if you substitute 
    $-k$ for $k$:

    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        & (-k)^3 - (-k) \\
        =& -(k^3) + k \\
        =& -(k^3 - k)
    \end{aligned}
    \]

    Let $f_k$ be the bi-infinite sequence $k^3 - k$ for all integers $k$. It turns out that rather than
    proving each term in this sequence is a multiple of $6$, it's easier to prove that
    \textit{the difference between consecutive terms} is a multiple of $6$. (Or at least
    \textit{I} found it to be.) Let's let $g_k$ be the difference between any $f_{k+1}$ and 
    $f_{k}$. That is,

    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        g_k &= f_{k+1} - f_{k} \\
        f_{k+1} &= f_k + g_k
    \end{aligned}
    \]

    Consider then that the sum of two multiples of $6$ is itself a multiple of $6$. I'll
    demonstrate this for two multiples of six $n = 6i$ and $m = 6j$, where $n$, $m$, $i$, and
    $j$ are all integers. Observe,

    \[
    \begin{aligned}
    n + m &= 6i + 6j \\
    &= 6(i + j)
    \end{aligned}
    \]

    Relying on the property that the sum of integers is an integer, we've shown that $n + m$
    can be expressed as a product of $6$ and some other integer $i + j$, and thus $n + m$ is
    a multiple of $6$.

    Thus, if I can show that every term in $g$ is a multiple of $6$, I can use mathematical
    induction to show that every term in $f$ is the sum of two multiples of $6$---in which case
    all terms of $f$ are themselves multiples of $6$.

    To start, I'll show that $g_k$ is a multiple of $6$ for 
    all integers $k$. Because $6$ is equal to $2 \times 3$, I can prove $g_k$ is a 
    multiple of $6$ by proving it is a multiple of both $2$ and $3$ for any integer $k$. 
    
    I can prove $g_k$ is always a multiple of $3$ by showing it can be 
    expressed as the product of $3$ and some other integer. Observe,

    \[
    \begin{aligned}
        g_k &= f_{k+1} - f_k \\
            &= (k+1)^3 - (k+1) - (k^3 - k) \\
            &= (k+1)\big((k+1)^2 - 1\big) - (k^3 - k) \\
            &= (k+1)(k^2 + 2k) - (k^3 - k) \\
            &= k^3 + 3k^2 + 2k - k^3 + k \\
            &= 3k^2 + 3k \\
            &= 3(k^2 + k)
    \end{aligned}
    \]

    To prove that $g_k$ is a multiple of $2$ for all integers $k$, I will prove that $g_k$ is
    \textit{even} for all integers $k$. Firstly, recognize that for any integer $k$, if 
    $k^2 + k$ is even, then $3(k^2 + k)$ is even. Then, recognize that for any \textit{even} 
    integer $k$, both $k^2$ and $k$ are even, and so their sum $k^2 + k$ is even. Lastly, recognize that
    for any \textit{odd} integer $k$, both $k^2$ and $k$ are odd, and so $k^2 + k$ is
    even. So, regardless of whether an integer $k$ is even or odd, $k^2 + k$ is even and 
    therefore $g_k = 3(k^2 - k)$ is also even. 
    
    (This line of reasoning relies on the knowledge that the product of an even and an odd is 
    even; the square of an even is even; the square
    of an odd is odd; the sum (or difference) of two evens is even; and the sum (or 
    difference) of two odds is even.)

    Therefore, having shown that $g_k$ is a multiple of $3$ and even for every integer $k$,
    I have proven that $g_k$ is a multiple of $6$ for every integer $k$.

    The rest can be proven by mathematical induction on $k$. Suppose that for some integer
    $k$, $f_k$ is a multiple of $6$. Consider then the case for $k+1$.
    Because $f_k$ and $g_k$ are both multiples of $6$, their sum, $f_{k+1}$, is also a
    multiple of $6$. Thus, the result holds for the inductive case.

    Finally, consider the base case $f_0$. Observe,

    \[\begin{aligned}
        f_0 &= (0)^3 - (0) \\
            &= 0
    \end{aligned}\]

    $0$ is a multiple of $6$ (it can be expressed as $6 \times 0$). Thus, the result holds for
    the base case $k = 0$. Then, by the principle of mathematical induction, $k^3 - k$ is
    a multiple of $6$ for every integer $k$.

\end{proof}

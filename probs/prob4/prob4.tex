\[
  F_n = \begin{cases}
    1                 & \text{if } n = 1 \\
    1                 & \text{if } n = 2 \\
    F_{n-1} + F_{n-2} & \text{if } n \ge 3
  \end{cases}
\]

\begin{proposition}
  \[
    \forall m \in \mathbb{N}, \sum_{j=1}^{m} F_{2j-1} = F_{2m}
  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof} The proof is by induction on $m$.

BASE CASE: Consider the case for $m=1$. Observe,

\[\begin{aligned}
  \sum_{j=1}^1 F_{2j-1} &= F_{2(1)-1} \\
                        &= F_{2-1} \\
                        &= F_1 \\
                        &= 1
\end{aligned}\]

and,

\[
  F_{2(1)} = F_2 = 1
\]

Thus, the result holds for the base case $m=1$.

INDUCTIVE CASE: Suppose the result holds for some natural number $k$. That is,

\[
  \sum_{j=1}^{k} F_{2j-1} = F_{2k}
\]

Now consider the case for $k+1$. Observe,

\[\begin{aligned}
  \sum_{j=1}^{k+1} F_{2j-1} &= \sum_{j=1}^k (F_{2j-1}) + F_{2(k+1)-1} \\
                   &= F_{2k} + F_{2(k+1)-1} \\
                   &= F_{2k} + F_{2k+1} \\
                   &= F_{2k+2} \\
                   &= F_{2(k+1)}
\end{aligned}\]

Let's pull back the curtain on some of the magic here. 
$\sum_{j=1}^k (F_{2j-1}) = F_{2k}$ by the inductive hypothesis.
And $F_{2k} + F_{2k+1} = F_{2k+2}$ by the definition of the Fibonacci sequence. We are allowed to do this substitution because the index $2k+2$ is always greater than 3, meaning that $F_{2k+2}$ sits within the $n > 3$ piece of $F_n$.

The result, $F_{2(k+1)}$ has the form $F_{2m}$ for $m = k+1$. Thus, the result holds in the inductive case. Therefore, by the principle of mathematical induction, the result holds for all $m \in \mathbb{N}$.

\end{proof}

  \]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof} The proof is by induction on $m$.
